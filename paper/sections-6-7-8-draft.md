# 6. Discussion

The Statistical Decision Tree addresses a recurring gap between learning statistical procedures and selecting, implementing, and reporting them in applied research. Earlier selection aids have shown the value of organizing decisions around the research question, measurement level, number of groups, dependence of observations, and distributional conditions (McCrum-Gardner, 2008; Parab & Bhalerao, 2010). Interactive systems such as StatXFinder further demonstrated that sequential questions, visible answers, and explanatory support can make this process more accessible (Suner et al., 2015). The present tool extends this approach by linking the decision path to example data, detailed assumptions, jamovi and R procedures, effect-size guidance, and reporting scaffolds within a multilingual interface.

This integration is the principal contribution of the project. Static diagrams provide a useful overview but usually end when a procedure has been named. The current application treats selection as one stage in a broader analytical workflow. Users can inspect why a method was recommended, reproduce it with the supplied dataset, compare graphical and script-based implementations, and identify the quantities needed for scientific reporting. Direct search also preserves the methodological context by reconstructing the path associated with the selected procedure.

The tool may serve complementary educational functions. Students can use the guided route to practise translating research designs into analytical decisions, while instructors can use the scenarios, datasets, and static overview diagrams in teaching. The parallel jamovi and R routes may support progression from an accessible graphical interface to a reproducible scripted workflow. For researchers, the application can function as an initial orientation and reporting checklist, particularly for procedures used less frequently. These proposed benefits remain to be tested empirically and should not be interpreted as demonstrated learning or decision-accuracy effects.

The application also makes the limits of automated selection visible. A procedure compatible with the selected answers is not necessarily optimal for a particular dataset. Measurement validity, sampling, missing data, multiplicity, statistical power, model specification, diagnostic results, and substantive theory may require decisions that cannot be represented adequately in a short tree. Similarly, regression, mediation, structural equation models, and machine-learning procedures do not establish causality through their use alone. The tool is therefore best understood as structured decision support rather than an automated statistical authority.

# 7. Limitations and Future Development

Several limitations should guide interpretation of the current beta version. First, the breadth of the tool requires simplification. Some endpoints represent families of models whose appropriate specification depends on details not captured by the preceding questions. The tree prioritizes common instructional routes and does not cover every estimator, design, diagnostic procedure, or field-specific convention.

Second, content verification is incomplete. The assumptions have undergone an internal structured review, and software instructions and datasets have been revised iteratively, but not every endpoint has received independent expert validation. The translations also require systematic review by native speakers with statistical expertise. jamovi menus and third-party modules may change, making procedures and screenshots vulnerable to version drift.

Third, the example datasets are teaching resources rather than validated benchmark datasets. Some are deliberately small or strongly structured to make a procedure easy to reproduce. They should not be used to infer realistic effect distributions, model performance, or required sample sizes. Screenshot coverage is also incomplete, and correspondence among datasets, software output, R code, and reporting examples should be checked again for each archived release.

Finally, the tool has not yet undergone a formal user study. Future evaluation should examine whether students and researchers can reach appropriate recommendations, explain the reasoning path, complete the analysis, and report the required statistics. Relevant outcomes include decision accuracy, task completion, completion time, usability, cognitive load, and qualitative feedback. Comparisons with a static decision chart or conventional reference material would help determine which benefits arise specifically from the interactive design.

Future releases should therefore prioritize independent statistical and linguistic review, automated tests for path and translation completeness, accessibility auditing, versioned jamovi and R verification, and empirical usability evaluation. A stable publication release should archive the source code, datasets, expected outputs, software versions, and decision-path inventory under a clear open license and persistent identifier.

# 8. Conclusion

The Statistical Decision Tree provides a multilingual, browser-based bridge between research questions and practical statistical workflows. It combines guided method selection with explicit assumptions, applied examples, downloadable data, jamovi and R instructions, effect-size guidance, and reporting scaffolds. Its purpose is not to replace statistical expertise, but to make the reasoning from study design to analysis more visible, teachable, and reproducible. Independent content validation and user evaluation are now required to establish the accuracy, usability, and educational value of this approach.
