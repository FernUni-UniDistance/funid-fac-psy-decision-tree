# Introduction Draft

Quantitative methods are central to research in psychology, sport science, and related behavioral disciplines. They are used not only to test differences and associations, but also to evaluate multivariable models, investigate latent structures, make predictions, and synthesize evidence across studies. The validity of the resulting conclusions depends, however, on whether the selected analytical procedure matches the research question, measurement characteristics, study design, dependency structure, and assumptions of the data. Selecting an analysis is therefore not simply a matter of identifying a familiar test name or choosing an option from a software menu. It is a sequence of linked methodological decisions that begins before the analysis is run and continues through model checking, interpretation, and reporting.

Learning to make these decisions is difficult. Students may become competent at calculating or executing a statistical procedure when the procedure has already been specified, yet struggle to recognize when it is appropriate in a new research situation. Gardner and Hudson (1999) described this as the development of statistical “selection skills”: the ability to connect features of a substantive problem with a suitable analytical procedure. Their investigation showed that even students who had completed statistics courses experienced difficulty identifying appropriate procedures from applied research scenarios. A later qualitative study with psychology students and academics similarly found that statistical decision making was challenging and was shaped by how research methods and statistics were taught (Allen et al., 2016). Participants emphasized the value of contextualized examples and decision support that makes the reasoning process visible. These findings suggest that statistical education should address not only how an analysis is performed, but also how researchers move from a research question and design to a defensible analytical choice.

The consequences of weak statistical decision making extend beyond the classroom. Reviews of biomedical research have documented errors at multiple stages, including study planning, selection and application of statistical tests, treatment of assumptions, interpretation, and reporting (Strasak et al., 2007; Thiese et al., 2015). In psychology, automated screening of more than three decades of published articles identified persistent inconsistencies between reported test statistics, degrees of freedom, and *p* values (Nuijten et al., 2016). Comparable concerns apply to sport and medical research, where the CHAMP statement highlights the need to evaluate design, analysis, reporting, presentation, and interpretation together rather than treating the statistical test as an isolated calculation (Mansournia et al., 2021). These problems cannot be solved by a decision tree alone, and complex or high-stakes analyses still require appropriate methodological expertise. Nevertheless, educational tools can help users formulate the right preliminary questions, recognize common assumptions, and retain the information needed for transparent reporting.

Reporting standards reinforce the importance of this broader workflow. The Journal Article Reporting Standards for Quantitative Research in Psychology recommend transparent descriptions of hypotheses, research design, analytical methods, effect estimates, uncertainty, and distinctions between primary, secondary, and exploratory analyses (Appelbaum et al., 2018). A statistically appropriate analysis can still be difficult to evaluate or reproduce when the decision process, data, code, model specification, or necessary results are unavailable. Reproducible workflows therefore connect analytical decisions with the data and commands that produced the reported findings. Script-based analyses and open data can allow an independent researcher to repeat an analysis and obtain the same computational results (LeBeau et al., 2021). For learners, combining explanation, example data, software procedures, and reporting guidance may also reduce the fragmentation between statistics courses, software training, and scientific writing.

Numerous flowcharts, selection tables, and online guides have been developed to support statistical test selection. Published examples commonly organize decisions around the research objective, level of measurement, number of groups or variables, independence of observations, and distributional conditions (e.g., McCrum-Gardner, 2008; Parab & Bhalerao, 2010). More recent frameworks have expanded this logic to a broader collection of regression, covariance, and multivariate procedures (Kim et al., 2024). These resources can provide valuable orientation, but static charts must compress complex decisions into a limited space and often end when a test name has been reached. Consequently, the user may still need separate resources to understand assumptions, locate the analysis in software, obtain suitable practice data, interpret an effect size, or write the result.

Interactive decision-support systems can reveal one question at a time and provide explanations without requiring the entire decision structure to fit on a single page. StatXFinder represented an important development in this direction. Suner et al. (2015) integrated ten earlier decision trees into a web-based system containing 85 statistical tests and 59 recommendations. Its questions and decision paths were reviewed by statistical experts, and a usability study with biomedical researchers showed the educational potential of stepwise explanations, backtracking, a glossary, and visible previous answers. The study also identified requests for multilingual support and broader multivariate coverage. Other resources have connected statistical selection to software. For example, the updated PowerPoint-based guide described by Torres and Normando (2021) provides versions in Portuguese, English, and Spanish and includes routes for conducting analyses in jamovi and other freely available software. Such tools demonstrate the value of accessible and software-oriented guidance, while also showing that test selection, practical implementation, reproducibility, and reporting are often distributed across different resources.

Open statistical environments offer an opportunity to connect these elements. jamovi provides a graphical interface that can make statistical analysis accessible to users who are not yet comfortable writing code, while its module system extends the available analyses (Sahin & Aybek, 2019). R complements this interface with transparent, scriptable workflows, and R Markdown can combine narrative explanation, code, and output in a reproducible document. A dual pathway can therefore support different levels of experience: a learner may begin by reproducing an analysis through a graphical interface and later inspect or adapt the corresponding code. Importantly, software accessibility does not itself guarantee an appropriate analysis. The educational value lies in linking software actions to the design decisions and assumptions that justify them.

To address these needs, we developed the Statistical Decision Tree, a free browser-based educational tool for students, instructors, and applied researchers in psychology, sport science, and adjacent behavioral fields. The application guides users through questions concerning the aim of the analysis, number and type of variables, measurement level, independent or repeated observations, distributional conditions, clustering, observed or latent variables, prediction goals, and the form of information available for meta-analysis. The current version covers classical hypothesis tests as well as regression, mixed models, exploratory and latent-variable procedures, machine-learning methods, and meta-analysis. Users may follow the guided tree or search directly for a method; a search selection reconstructs the corresponding path so that the methodological conditions remain visible.

At each recommendation endpoint, the tool brings together a rationale, expandable assumption guidance, examples from psychology and sport science, a downloadable dataset, jamovi instructions and available screenshots, R code, effect-size guidance, and an APA-style reporting scaffold. A companion R Markdown project provides executable analyses using the example datasets. The interface is available in English, German, French, Spanish, and Italian. The application is explicitly intended as decision support: it does not determine whether a research design permits causal inference, replace diagnostic evaluation, or remove the need for statistical consultation in complex studies.

The present tutorial has three aims. First, it describes the methodological and educational principles used to translate features of a research question and dataset into an interactive decision path. Second, it demonstrates how the tool connects method selection with assumptions, example data, implementation in jamovi and R, effect-size interpretation, and scientific reporting. Third, it documents the development and verification workflow and identifies priorities for external content review, usability evaluation, and future expansion. By making the path from research question to reported result inspectable and reproducible, the project seeks to support statistical reasoning rather than merely automate test selection.
